Strategic Overview

Houthi Maritime Campaign — Doctrine and Impact

Beginning in November 2023, the Houthi movement (Ansar Allah) launched a sustained maritime interdiction campaign against commercial shipping in the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, and Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, explicitly linking the campaign to the conflict in Gaza and declaring targeting of vessels with Israeli connections or those bound for Israeli ports.

The campaign has evolved into the most significant disruption to global maritime trade since the 2021 Ever Given Suez Canal blockage — and unlike that event, it is indefinite in duration. Approximately 12% of global trade by volume and 30% of global container trade transits the Red Sea. The practical effect has been a permanent rerouting of a large proportion of global container shipping via the Cape of Good Hope, adding approximately 10 days and 30% to freight costs.

Strategic Assessment — CENTCOM / DOD
The Houthi campaign represents the first sustained use of anti-ship ballistic missiles (ASBMs) by a non-state actor in combat, and the first operational deployment of one-way attack drones at scale against commercial shipping. The cost-exchange ratio strongly favors the Houthis — a $2,000 drone can force deployment of a $2M+ interceptor missile. The campaign has demonstrated that relatively low-cost Iranian proxy forces can impose significant costs on global commerce and on U.S. naval assets. [1][2]

Iran Proxy Relationship
The Houthi movement is a member of Iran's "Axis of Resistance" alongside Hezbollah, Hamas, and Iraqi militia groups. U.S. and allied assessments confirm Iran provides anti-ship missiles, drones, targeting intelligence, and technical advisors. The campaign serves Iranian strategic interests — demonstrating the reach of the proxy network, raising costs for U.S. naval presence, and creating leverage in nuclear negotiations — while maintaining deniability. Incident History

Key Events — November 2023 to Present

Nov 2023
Galaxy Leader Seizure — Campaign Opens. Houthi forces seized the vehicle carrier MV Galaxy Leader in the Red Sea using a helicopter insertion team, taking 25 crew members hostage. The vessel had a partial Israeli ownership connection. Marked the opening of the systematic maritime interdiction campaign and established the precedent for vessel seizure. Crew held for over a year. [1]
Dec 2023
Major Carriers Suspend Red Sea Transits. Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, and Evergreen suspended Red Sea transits within weeks of each other following multiple attacks. This represented the majority of global container capacity. Suez Canal transits fell sharply. Cape of Good Hope rerouting became the default for major carriers. [4]
Dec 2023
Operation Prosperity Guardian — Coalition Formed. U.S. Central Command announced Operation Prosperity Guardian, a multinational naval coalition to escort commercial shipping and intercept Houthi attacks in the Red Sea. Initial participants included UK, France, Bahrain, and others. USS Dwight D. Eisenhower carrier strike group deployed. [2]
Jan 2024
U.S. / UK Strikes on Houthi Targets. U.S. and UK forces conducted the first strikes on Houthi military infrastructure in Yemen following continued attacks on shipping. Strikes targeted radar systems, missile storage, and launch sites. Houthi attacks continued and escalated following the strikes, demonstrating resilience of dispersed capability. [2]
Feb 2024
MV Rubymar — First Vessel Sunk. The UK-registered bulk carrier MV Rubymar was struck by Houthi missiles and sank in the Red Sea — the first commercial vessel sunk in the campaign. The vessel was carrying fertilizer and created an oil spill and navigation hazard. Crew evacuated. [1][4]
Jun 2024
MV Tutor — Crew Killed. The Greek-owned bulk carrier MV Tutor was struck and sank following a Houthi attack, with one crew member killed — the first fatality directly attributed to the campaign. The sinking was attributed to a combination of missile and possible underwater explosive or mine. [1][4]
2024–25
Campaign Sustained — Scope Expanded. Houthi attacks continued at a rate of multiple incidents per week through 2024 and into 2025, expanding targeting criteria beyond Israeli-linked vessels to include U.S. and UK-flagged ships and vessels of any flag calling at U.S. or UK ports. Attack sophistication increased with coordinated multi-vector strikes combining ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones simultaneously. [1][2]

Coalition Response

Operation Prosperity Guardian

The U.S.-led multinational naval task force (CTF 153) operates under Operation Prosperity Guardian to protect commercial shipping and intercept Houthi attacks. The coalition has intercepted hundreds of drones and missiles but has not suppressed the campaign. The cost-exchange asymmetry — cheap Houthi weapons vs. expensive interceptors — has driven significant debate about the sustainability of the naval response.

Cost-Exchange Asymmetry
U.S. SM-2 and SM-6 interceptor missiles cost $2–4 million each. Houthi one-way attack drones cost an estimated $2,000–20,000 each. The economic model strongly favors the attacker. U.S. Navy ships have expended hundreds of interceptors; the Houthis have shown no meaningful reduction in attack tempo. Resolution requires either a diplomatic settlement of the Gaza conflict or a fundamental degradation of Houthi missile and drone production/supply infrastructure. [2]
