A New Drug Is Changing What’s Possible for Most Pancreatic Cancer Patients

What Happened This Week

On April 13, 2026, Revolution Medicines announced that a drug called daraxonrasib had passed its Phase 3 clinical trial — the largest, most rigorous kind of trial — in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer who had already received chemotherapy. Today, May 31, the full results are being formally presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology annual meeting in Chicago by Dr Brian Wolpin of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute at Harvard.

Why This Matters

Until now, no targeted drug had ever been shown to help patients with pancreatic cancer live longer in a large, rigorous clinical trial. One drug — olaparib, approved in 2019 for a small group of patients with a specific gene mutation — did slow the cancer down, but did not extend overall survival. For patients whose cancer had already been treated with chemotherapy and stopped responding, the best available options typically extended life by about 6 to 7 months on average.

Daraxonrasib changed that. In the RASolute 302 trial, patients who took it lived a median of 13.2 months — nearly twice as long as those on chemotherapy. If these results hold up as the full data are reviewed, this would be one of the biggest steps forward in pancreatic cancer treatment in many years.

The Science — Explained Simply

What is KRAS, and why does it matter for pancreatic cancer?

Every cell in your body has genes that control how the cell grows and divides. One of the most important is a gene called KRAS. In a healthy cell, KRAS acts like a light switch — it turns on to signal the cell to divide, and then turns off again.

In more than 90% of pancreatic cancers, the KRAS gene is mutated — it gets stuck in the ON position and never turns off. This means the cancer cells receive a constant signal to grow and multiply, which is what makes pancreatic cancer so aggressive. [PMC11055996]

Plain Language

Think of KRAS like a gas pedal in a car. In normal cells, you press it when you need to go and release it when you need to stop. In pancreatic cancer, the gas pedal gets jammed down permanently. The cell never stops accelerating.

Daraxonrasib is a drug designed to reach into that mechanism and release the stuck pedal.

Why did it take so long to find a drug that targets KRAS?

Scientists identified KRAS as a driver of cancer in human cells in 1982. For over 30 years, drug developers tried and failed to block it. The KRAS protein binds to GTP with extremely high affinity, and its surface lacks the deep binding pockets that most small molecule drugs require. The scientific and pharmaceutical communities largely concluded that KRAS could not be targeted directly.

That changed in 2013 when a team at the University of California San Francisco identified a small groove — called the switch II pocket — that is accessible on the G12C mutant form of KRAS when the protein is in its inactive state. This opened the door to a new generation of drugs — and eventually to daraxonrasib.

Does This Apply to You? — Understanding Your Test Results

If you have pancreatic cancer, your tumour may have been tested for gene mutations. The most important thing to understand is which KRAS mutation your tumour carries — because different drugs target different mutations.

Ask Your Doctor

If you have not had molecular testing of your tumour, ask your oncologist about it. Knowing your KRAS mutation status determines which treatments you may be eligible for — including clinical trials.

Mutation How Common in Pancreatic Cancer What It Means for Treatment
KRAS G12D 35–44% of patients Targeted by daraxonrasib (Phase 3 ✓) and zoldonrasib (Phase 1 — promising early results). Most common mutation in pancreatic cancer.
KRAS G12V 20–34% of patients Targeted by daraxonrasib (Phase 3 ✓). Second most common.
KRAS G12R 10–20% of patients Targeted by daraxonrasib (Phase 3 ✓).
KRAS G12C 1–3% of patients Targeted by sotorasib and adagrasib — both FDA approved in lung cancer, active in Phase 1/2 for pancreatic cancer.

Important: the Phase 3 daraxonrasib trial enrolled patients with all KRAS mutations — not just one specific type. This is significant because it means the majority of pancreatic cancer patients could potentially benefit, not just those with a rare subtype.

If your tumour has a KRAS G12C mutation or is KRAS wild-type (no KRAS mutation), other targeted options and trials exist — ask your oncologist specifically about those and whether additional molecular tests are appropriate for your situation.

What Does This Mean for You?

This depends on where you are in your treatment journey. Three situations:

If you have already had first-line chemotherapy and your cancer has progressed

This is the patient population that the Phase 3 daraxonrasib trial enrolled. The drug is not yet FDA approved. Revolution Medicines has announced its intention to submit a New Drug Application to the FDA under the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher pathway — a programme designed to accelerate review of treatments for high-priority health conditions. The FDA approved a two-day turnaround on Revolution Medicines' expanded access request on April 30, 2026, signalling urgency. No NDA submission date or expected approval date has been publicly announced by the company or the FDA as of this report.

ⓘ Sources: Revolution Medicines press release April 13, 2026 — NDA intent under CNPV; FDA.gov May 1, 2026 — expanded access safe-to-proceed letter; OncLive May 2026 — CNPV pathway and Breakthrough Therapy Designation; PanCAN pancan.org April 2026 — expanded access programme announcement. No publicly stated NDA submission date or approval date as of May 31, 2026.  ·  Sources verified May 31, 2026

In the meantime, ask your oncologist whether you are eligible for a clinical trial of daraxonrasib or zoldonrasib. Clinical trials are often the best way to access new drugs before approval.

If you are currently on first-line chemotherapy

Daraxonrasib is also being studied as a first-line treatment — before chemotherapy. Early data presented at the AACR 2026 conference showed activity in previously untreated patients. A separate Phase 3 first-line trial for zoldonrasib in G12D-mutated PDAC — called RASolute 305 — was initiated in early 2026. Your oncologist can tell you whether any first-line clinical trial might be appropriate for your situation.

ⓘ Sources: OncLive May 2026 — daraxonrasib first-line AACR 2026 data; Revolution Medicines February 25, 2026 financial results (SEC filing) — RASolute 305 initiated for zoldonrasib first-line G12D PDAC; ClinicalTrials.gov  ·  Sources verified May 31, 2026

If you have just been diagnosed

Ask your oncologist to arrange molecular testing of your tumour. Knowing your KRAS mutation status before you start any treatment gives you the most options — including eligibility for targeted therapies and clinical trials that may not be available without a confirmed mutation profile. PanCAN's free Know Your Tumor® programme can arrange comprehensive molecular testing and help interpret the results in the context of available treatments.

The Most Important Thing You Can Do Today

Ask your oncologist: "Has my tumour been tested for KRAS mutations, and am I eligible for any clinical trials of KRAS inhibitors?" If you have not had molecular testing, ask for it specifically. If your oncologist is not familiar with these trials, a second opinion at a National Cancer Institute (NCI) designated comprehensive cancer centre can help — find an NCI cancer centre near you.

Questions to Ask Your Oncologist

  • Has my tumour been tested for KRAS mutations? What mutation do I have?
  • Am I eligible for daraxonrasib — either through the expanded access programme now, through a clinical trial, or once it receives FDA approval?
  • Is there a clinical trial of a KRAS inhibitor I could join at this stage of my treatment?
  • Should I be seen at an NCI-designated comprehensive cancer centre that specialises in pancreatic cancer?
  • What is my BRCA status? Olaparib (Lynparza) is FDA approved since December 2019 as maintenance treatment for germline BRCA-mutated metastatic pancreatic cancer that has not progressed on at least 16 weeks of first-line platinum chemotherapy.
  • Are there any other molecular markers in my tumour — MSI-H, NTRK, HER2 — that might open additional treatment options?

What Is a Clinical Trial?

A clinical trial is a carefully supervised research study that tests whether a new treatment is safe and effective. Trials have phases:

  • Phase 1 — a small number of patients, focused on safety and finding the right dose. Often the first time a drug has been given to humans.
  • Phase 2 — a larger group, looking at whether the drug works and gathering more safety data.
  • Phase 3 — a large, randomised trial comparing the new drug directly against the current standard of care. This is what daraxonrasib just completed — and passed.

Joining a clinical trial is not a last resort. For many patients with pancreatic cancer, a clinical trial is the best available option — it provides access to drugs that are not yet commercially available and is supervised by specialists who focus specifically on your cancer type.

To find trials you may be eligible for, visit clinicaltrials.gov and search for "pancreatic cancer KRAS." You can filter by your location and stage.
